## 2. Full Report

### 2.1 Introduction & Definition

The excerpts define emotional programming as a relational, transgenerational learning process. Its vehicle is relationship and learning, explicitly "not genes" [[1]], and its content is conveyed through "verbal, nonverbal, and behavioral interactions" within and between generations [[1]]. Kerr's definition is developmental and interactional: it "occurs through the ongoing interactions parents and other family members have with a child during his developmental years," with the possibility that "[s]ome programming may occur during intrauterine development" [[4, 6]]. A third formulation emphasizes the effect on self-development: it "refers to the specific effects that the nature of the parents' interactions with a child during development have on the child's ability to separate emotionally and develop a self" [[7]].

A terminological distinction is drawn between content and process: "behavioral transmission usually refers to the content of the information transmitted across generations (such as a specific skill or belief) and emotional programming refers to a process between the generations" [[2, 3]]. Emotional programming is also described as one of "three Es of Bowen theory: emotional regression, emotional objectivity, and emotional programming," which are grouped because "each concept is unique to Bowen theory," the term "emotional" being placed in front "to anchor them to the bridge—the emotional system—that Bowen theory constructs to link human behavior to evolution and the behavior of other species" [[11]].

### 2.2 Theoretical Foundations

The excerpts place emotional programming inside the multigenerational transmission process. Bowen theory's concept of that process "is broader than what biologists generally mean by behavioral transmission," in that it "includes the emotional programming of one generation by the generations preceding it," and the programming is described as "the basis for a behavioral inheritance system that seems as precise and predictable in the transmission of information across generations as the genetic inheritance system" [[3]]. Emotional programming is named as "an important process to consider for understanding the transmission of information across generations" and as one that "may also provide a link between role differentiation and level of differentiation" [[5, 10]].

The sources note the concept's thin elaboration in Bowen's own writing: Bowen "only used 'emotional(ly) program(med)' twice in his collected papers" and wrote that the multigenerational transmission process was the "least detailed" and "needs the most attention" [[15, 17]]. The Bowen quotation given is: "Each person is programmed from birth to serve a certain set of functions and each 'senses' what is required or expected, more from the way the system functions around him than from verbal messages that he is free to function as he pleases" [[15, 17]]. Multigenerational emotional process is described as "anchored in the emotional system and includes emotions, feelings, and subjectively determined attitudes, values, and beliefs that are transmitted from one generation to the next," with transmission "assumed to occur primarily through relationships" [[15, 17]].

On the relation to biology, one excerpt notes that the "exploding field of epigenetics, which examines the interplay between the environment and the switching off and on of genes, opens the door to studying the interaction between emotional programming and gene expression," while stating that research "is at too early a stage to form firm conclusions about such a process" [[1]]. Related hypotheses appear elsewhere: that "[t]he increased social tolerance might involve a relationship-induced expression of previously unexpressed genes" [[12, 14]], and that relationship-system regulation of gene expression is "consistent with current knowledge in biology" while transmission of programming with fidelity to future generations is "a hypothesis that treads the borders of current knowledge in biology" [[27, 29]].

### 2.3 Key Dimensions

**Mechanism and channels.** Programming flows through verbal, nonverbal, and behavioral interaction [[1]]; through ongoing interaction with parents and other family members during developmental years, possibly beginning in utero [[4, 6]]; and through reciprocal triggering, since "[t]he child triggers emotionally programmed reactions in the parent simultaneously with the parent triggering emotional programming in the child" [[9]].

**Sites of intensity.** "The most intense programming occurs in a person's parental triangle during development, but siblings and other family members have an important influence on it as well" [[1]]. "Differences in the intensity of family triangles result in siblings being programmed differently" [[4, 6]].

**Levels of functioning affected.** Programming "appears to affect a child's biochemical, physiological, psychological, and relationship functioning" [[4, 6]]; it "involves cortical and subcortical processes" [[23]]; and it is "anchored in the emotional system but includes complex feeling and psychological components" [[4, 6]]. "Automatic responses have interconnected emotional, feeling, and subjective cognitive components" [[1]].

**Consciousness.** "People are not conscious of most of the emotional programming that occurs, but it affects myriad automatic behaviors that govern much of people's lives, for better and for worse," and "the programming can intensify or deintensify fear reactions to social stimuli" [[1]].

**Specific social cues.** Four "social cues" are named as "consistent elements of the programming that occurs": "parental attention, approval, expectations, and distress," with heightened reactivity to these cues constraining "the child's ability to formulate thoughts, opinions, and ideas on their own" [[7]].

**Individuality–togetherness balance.** "Emotional programming occurs in the context of the interplay between the counterbalancing life forces of individuality and togetherness in parent-offspring relationships. Variations in the particular balance of these forces can explain variation in the intensity of the emotional programming that occurs, as well as variation in the capacity for self-regulation" [[9]].

**Reactivity and response range.** Programming "labels certain behaviors as good and to be fostered and others as bad and to be discouraged," leaving the individual, "depending on his reactivity in the system," with responses "on the spectrum from over-compliance to counter-positioning" [[16]].

### 2.4 Relationship to Other Bowen Concepts

- **Differentiation of self.** Different children are "more or less emotionally involved in the family emotional processes, resulting in the children having different levels of differentiation of self" [[13, 17]]; and one hypothesis states that "level of differentiation is created by multigenerational emotional programming" [[22]].
- **Anxiety.** "Chronic anxiety drives the more intense focus, not the reality needs of the child" [[4, 6]]; and among "the various variables that have to do with the programming of people" are "environmental family attitudes about right and wrong" and messages conveying "you are the one who is the cause of the problem," alongside the degree of differentiation of self and degree of anxiety [[21]].
- **Triangles.** The parental triangle is named as the most intense site [[1]]; the intensity of family triangles accounts for differential programming of siblings [[4, 6]]; and the degree of fusion in a parental triangle "cannot be explained adequately by looking at just two generations" [[9]].
- **Fusion.** Fusion with a dysfunctional mother "programs the child into a strong interdependence with the mother, showing itself in strong need for her attention and later in romantic relationships" [[26]].
- **Emotional regression and emotional objectivity.** Grouped with emotional programming as the "three Es" [[11]].
- **Evolution and nonhuman comparison.** Emotional programming is discussed in relation to species differences in social organization, naked mole-rats, prairie voles, and Calhoun's rat experiments [[12, 14, 22, 27, 29, 30]].
- **Theory of Constructed Emotion (as one author's extension).** Galloway proposes that the ToCE "offers some interesting ideas with respect to the concept of multigenerational transmission process and emotional programming," while stating that it "misses the important idea of how, what, and when does one learn about how they should function in their family" [[8, 18]]; she suggests "the sensing of how one should function becomes one's goals," with most of this learning "nonconscious" [[19]].

### 2.5 Clinical Presentation

The excerpts give several illustrations. A mother "fiercely protective" of a rebellious adolescent son, acting from "a deep yearning to be able to make him happy," overfunctions with him; the son reports being hassled, fights her "on most every issue," and sometimes pushes her away physically; the source states that "[t]his is the type of interaction that emotionally programs the son," programming him "to be hypersensitive to criticism and for his first response to be oppositional," with the mother's programming relating "in part, to her matching her mother's worry about her younger brother and feeling sorry for him" [[20]]. A second pattern involves a dysfunctional mother, "perhaps chronically depressed or abusing substances," with a child "highly tuned into the mother's moods and feeling a lot of responsibility for making her happy," "sort of like a parent-focused child"; the intensity of that fusion programs "a strong interdependence with the mother," with the child sensitized to the mother's periods of withdrawal [[26]]. A third describes a mother investing anxiously in her children, producing "a heightened need for attention in the child," entanglement, and a child whose "sense of emotional well-being becomes more dependent than it would otherwise have been on what the mother does and says," a pattern that "transfers into other relationships" [[23]]. The sources also note that parents "commonly say they are more involved with or worry more about one child because that child has more problems or needs but miss how they unwittingly program the child to need more of their energy and attention. Their unrealistic needs for and expectations of the child program him to have unrealistic needs for and expectations of them" [[4, 6]].

### 2.6 Clinical Implications & Therapeutic Approach

The excerpts state that seeing the multigenerational process "provides a much-needed broad perspective for what is unfolding in the here and now" [[9]], and that "[t]he degree of fusion in a parental triangle cannot be explained adequately by looking at just two generations" [[9]]. The therapeutic orientation quoted is Bowen's saying: "That which was created in a relationship [emotional programming in the relationship with his mother] can be fixed in a relationship [in the relationship with his wife]," with the observation that "[t]he success of a Bowen-theory-based approach depends being able to change in relationship to important others in the real world" [[25]]. In a personal vignette, thinking of his mother "as a child of her mother, not just as my Mother" helped the author "be less judgmental and more tolerant of certain traits in Mother that also affected my development," and he asks: "What choice did Mother have about the nature of her emotional programming?" [[24]]. Guerin notes that "[i]t is this emotional programming that those of us who choose the mental health profession bring into a training context," where trainees receive signals that "pre-programmed, natural behaviors are to be left behind and a new set of behaviors learned," and "[t]his new programming then will allow the trainee to do it the 'right way'" [[16]].
