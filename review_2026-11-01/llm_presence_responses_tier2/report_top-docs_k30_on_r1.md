## 1. Executive Summary

Within the excerpts provided, emotional programming is described as a process between the generations that occurs through relationships and learning rather than through genes [[1, 4]]. Kerr draws an explicit distinction: "behavioral transmission usually refers to the content of the information transmitted across generations (such as a specific skill or belief) and emotional programming refers to a process between the generations" [[1]]. The phenomenon is said to occur "through the transmission of information across generations in a family by relationships and learning, not genes" and to result "from the verbal, nonverbal, and behavioral interactions that occur within and between the generations" [[4]].

The excerpts locate emotional programming within Bowen theory as one of a set of core emotional processes. One source names it first among "three phenomena or emotional processes described by Bowen theory" — emotional programming, emotional regression, and individuality and togetherness — and describes it as important "for understanding the transmission of information across generations," possibly providing "a link between role differentiation and level of differentiation" [[2]]. Another source groups it with emotional regression and emotional objectivity as "the three Es of Bowen theory," asserting that "each concept is unique to Bowen theory" and that "no other theory in psychology or biology has anything comparable" [[19]].

A second, more clinical definition is offered: "Emotional programming refers to the specific effects that the nature of the parents' interactions with a child during development have on the child's ability to separate emotionally and develop a self" [[7]]. This is described as reciprocal: the more parents focus on a child out of their own needs and fears, the more the child focuses automatically on the parents, heightening the child's reactivity to "parental attention, approval, expectations, and distress" — the four "social cues" named as consistent elements of the programming [[7]]. Most programming is said to be outside awareness, occurring most intensely in the parental triangle during development, while still being influenced by siblings and other family members, and affecting "myriad automatic behaviors" [[4]].

The excerpts connect emotional programming to emotional fusion and the multigenerational transmission process [[27]], to the family projection process and the focused-on child [[15]], to symptom development [[3]], to chronic anxiety [[5, 12]], and to the automatic ways of perceiving, feeling, and behaving an individual brings from the multigenerational family into the nuclear family [[17]]. Sources differ in how well specified the mechanism is: one states that "the exact ways in which this 'programming' occurs are not well defined" [[5]], while another details its channels as verbal, nonverbal, and behavioral interaction [[4]].

## 2. Full Report

### 2.1 Introduction & Definition

The excerpts provided do not present a single unified statement of the concept; they offer several descriptions. The definition emphasising transmission reads: "programming is the basis for a behavioral inheritance system that seems as precise and predictable in the transmission of information across generations as the genetic inheritance system," with behavioral transmission referring to content and emotional programming referring to "a process between the generations" [[1]]. A companion passage states that "the phenomenon of emotional programming occurs through the transmission of information across generations in a family by relationships and learning, not genes" [[4]].

The definition emphasising the parent–child interaction reads: "Emotional programming refers to the specific effects that the nature of the parents' interactions with a child during development have on the child's ability to separate emotionally and develop a self" [[7]].

The excerpts also state that the programming "can intensify or deintensify fear reactions to social stimuli" and that "automatic responses have interconnected emotional, feeling, and subjective cognitive components" [[4]]. It is described as resulting from "verbal, nonverbal, and behavioral interactions" [[4]] and, in another source, as occurring through "multiple verbal and non-verbal message[s]" that label "certain behaviors as good and to be fostered and others as bad and to be discouraged" [[13]].

### 2.2 Theoretical Foundations

Emotional programming is placed among the processes Bowen theory uses to explain transmission across generations [[2]]. It is described as one of "three phenomena or emotional processes described by Bowen theory," the others being emotional regression and individuality and togetherness [[2]]. A different grouping appears in another source, which calls "emotional regression, emotional objectivity, and emotional programming" the "three Es of Bowen theory" [[19]]. The two excerpts therefore group the concept differently; the sources do not reconcile these groupings.

The concept is presented as distinctive to Bowen theory: "No other theory in psychology or biology has anything comparable. The terms regression, objectivity, and programming are used in other bodies of knowledge, but not in the same way Bowen theory uses them," with the word "emotional" anchoring each term "to the bridge — the emotional system — that Bowen theory constructs to link human behavior to evolution and the behavior of other species" [[19]].

The excerpts place emotional programming in an evolutionary frame: it is offered as a phenomenon relevant to understanding "the transmission of information across generations" and as a possible link "between role differentiation and level of differentiation" [[2]]. In the account of fox domestication, the changes involved "both changes in gene functioning and in learning, both of which derived from their relationship interaction with human beings — or what Bowen theory refers to as emotional programming" [[18]].

Regarding epigenetics, one source states that "the exploding field of epigenetics, which examines the interplay between the environment and the switching off and on of genes, opens the door to studying the interaction between emotional programming and gene expression," but that "the research in this field is at too early a stage to form firm conclusions about such a process" [[4]].

### 2.3 Key Dimensions

**Channels and locus.** Programming results from "the verbal, nonverbal, and behavioral interactions that occur within and between the generations"; "the most intense programming occurs in a person's parental triangle during development, but siblings and other family members have an important influence on it as well" [[4]].

**Awareness.** "People are not conscious of most of the emotional programming that occurs, but it affects myriad automatic behaviors that govern much of people's lives, for better and for worse" [[4]].

**Reciprocity and reactivity.** The process is "reciprocal and ongoing," "can be harmonious or contentious," and "heightens the child's reactivity to parental attention, approval, expectations, and distress" — the four consistent "social cues" [[7]]. Anxiety-driven parental overinvolvement "not only heightens his or her reactivity to the cues, but it also constrains the child's ability to formulate thoughts, opinions, and ideas on their own," and heightened reactivity to approval, "coupled with unsureness about one's own ideas (or the reactive opposite, which is arrogance)," is described as a consequence [[7]].

**Content-labelling and response range.** Programming through verbal and non-verbal messages "labels certain behaviors as good and to be fostered and others as bad and to be discouraged," leaving the individual with "multiple responses on the spectrum from over-compliance to counter-positioning," which may vary across the life cycle [[13]].

**Biological/psychological programming of anxiety.** People "can be 'programmed' on biological and psychological levels to have a certain level of anxiety"; "some 'programming' may occur in the womb" based on the physiological mother–fetus relationship, "most of it occurs while children are growing up," and "the exact ways in which this 'programming' occurs are not well defined" [[5]]. Relatedly, absorption of anxiety "seems to be based on the physiological programming or conditioning of one person by another that occurs through prolonged association, and on the imparting and incorporation of attitudes and beliefs that foster anxiety in oneself or others" [[12]].

### 2.4 Relationship to Other Bowen Concepts

- **Emotional fusion / basic level of differentiation:** a child's basic level "is promoted or constrained by the degree of emotional fusion that exists between parents and child and the associated emotional programming that occurs within the fusion" [[27]].
- **Multigenerational transmission process:** emotional programming is discussed as part of understanding this process [[2, 27]]; one source describes seeing "the subtle and obvious programming occurring through the generations and determining so much of human behavior" [[28]].
- **Family projection process / focused-on child:** the child most targeted by parental anxious interaction "inherits the parents' immaturities through what Bowen theory terms emotional programming" [[15]]; in the author's family example, the legacy of an emotionally intense interactive process was "emotional programming of a heightened sensitivity to the feelings of others and feelings of guilt," which "programmed into my brother a very strong dependency on our mother to help him to manage his feeling states, which is part of what erodes people's 'self'" [[8]].
- **Nuclear family emotional system:** each individual "brings emotional programming or automatic ways of perceiving, feeling, and behaving from his or her experience in the multigenerational family into his or her nuclear family, such as projecting versus absorbing tension in the triangle" [[17]].
- **Symptom development:** symptom evolution is said to have to do with "the kind of programming that went into these two people" and "the programming from the past" [[3]].
- **Emotional regression / objectivity:** grouped together as the three Es [[19]]; the excerpts do not elaborate the relationship among the three beyond that grouping.

### 2.5 Clinical Presentation

The excerpts describe programming appearing as heightened reactivity to attention and approval, unsureness about one's own ideas, dependency on another to manage feeling states, guilt at not relieving others' distress, heightened sensitivity to others' feelings, and a sense of well-being "excessively regulated by the relationship," which "transfers into other relationships" [[7, 8, 21]]. One source states that when a mother invests anxiously, the child "becomes addicted to attention and thus can be quick to feel inadequately attended to" [[21]]. Programming is also described as carrying attitudes formed by one's functional position in the family, as in Wanda's "emotional programming in her functional oldest position" [[14]]. The excerpts also refer to "Mother's emotional programming" and "Dad's emotional programming" in the author's own family [[11]].

### 2.6 Clinical Implications & Therapeutic Approach

The excerpts contain limited material on technique directed at emotional programming. One source reports Bowen saying, "That which was created in a relationship [emotional programming in the relationship with his mother] can be fixed in a relationship [in the relationship with his wife]," and states that "the success of a Bowen-theory-based approach depends being able to change in relationship to important others in the real world" [[16]]. Another source describes training contexts in which "pre-programmed, natural behaviors are to be left behind and a new set of behaviors learned," so that "this new programming then will allow the trainee to do it the 'right way'" [[13]]. Beyond these, the provided sources do not address specific therapeutic procedures for emotional programming.
