### 1. Executive Summary

Across the excerpts provided, "emotional programming" is presented as a distinctly Bowen-theory concept. One source groups it with emotional regression and emotional objectivity as "the three Es of Bowen theory," noting that although the words regression, objectivity and programming are used in other bodies of knowledge, they are not used in the same way, and that placing "emotional" in front of each term anchors them to the emotional system, the "bridge" Bowen theory constructs to link human behaviour to evolution and the behaviour of other species [[10]].

The sources describe emotional programming as a process rather than a content: behavioural transmission usually refers to the content of information transmitted across generations (such as a specific skill or belief), whereas emotional programming refers to a process between the generations [[2, 4]]. It is said to occur "through the transmission of information across generations in a family by relationships and learning, not genes," and to result from verbal, nonverbal and behavioral interactions within and between generations [[1]]. It is described as the basis for a behavioural inheritance system that seems as precise and predictable as the genetic inheritance system [[2, 4]].

The most intense programming is said to occur in a person's parental triangle during development, with siblings and other family members also influential [[1]]; differences in the intensity of family triangles are said to result in siblings being programmed differently, with chronic anxiety rather than the child's reality needs driving the more intense focus [[5, 7]]. Programming is described as largely outside awareness and as affecting myriad automatic behaviours [[1]], and as affecting a child's biochemical, physiological, psychological and relationship functioning [[5, 7]]. One definition emphasises the specific effects of the nature of parents' interactions on the child's ability to separate emotionally and develop a self [[6]]. The concept is tied to the multigenerational transmission process [[4, 16, 17]], to individuality and togetherness [[8, 14]], to level of differentiation [[21]], and to speculation about evolution and nonhuman species [[12, 14, 21, 26, 27]]. Clinical illustrations include a mother and her rebellious adolescent son [[19]] and a dysfunctional mother with a highly attuned child [[29]]. Sources state that what was created in a relationship can be fixed in a relationship [[25]].

### 2. Full Report

#### 1. Introduction & Definition

The excerpts do not offer a single uniform definition. One describes emotional programming as occurring "through the transmission of information across generations in a family by relationships and learning, not genes," resulting from verbal, nonverbal and behavioral interactions within and between generations [[1]]. Another states that emotional programming "refers to the specific effects that the nature of the parents' interactions with a child during development have on the child's ability to separate emotionally and develop a self" [[6]]. A third defines it by contrast with behavioural transmission: behavioural transmission "usually refers to the content of the information transmitted across generations (such as a specific skill or belief) and emotional programming refers to a process between the generations" [[2, 4]].

Several sources present it as a process within Bowen theory's account of multigenerational transmission: "The multigenerational transmission process includes the emotional programming of one generation by the generations preceding it" [[4]]. It is named as the first of "three phenomena or emotional processes described by Bowen theory" to be discussed, and is said to be "an important process to consider for understanding the transmission of information across generations" and one that "may also provide a link between role differentiation and level of differentiation" [[3, 11]].

Terminology should be noted. Excerpts [[28]] and [[30]] do not address emotional programming; they use "program"/"programs" for institutional programmes [[28]] and "emotionality" in work systems [[30]], which the excerpts do not equate with emotional programming.

#### 2. Theoretical Foundations

The sources place emotional programming inside Bowen theory's multigenerational framework. Bowen theory's concept of a multigenerational transmission process is described as "broader than what biologists generally mean by behavioral transmission" [[4]]. Multigenerational emotional process is described as anchored in the emotional system and as including "emotions, feelings, and subjectively determined attitudes, values, and beliefs that are transmitted from one generation to the next," with this transmission "assumed to occur primarily through relationships" [[16, 17]]. The same source notes that Bowen only used "emotional(ly) program(med)" twice in his collected papers and wrote that the multigenerational transmission process was the "least detailed" and "needs the most attention" [[16, 17]], quoting: "Each person is programmed from birth to serve a certain set of functions and each 'senses' what is required or expected, more from the way the system functions around him than from verbal messages that he is free to function as he pleases" [[16, 17]].

On genetics, one source is explicit that programming occurs "by relationships and learning, not genes," while noting that epigenetics "opens the door to studying the interaction between emotional programming and gene expression," with research "at too early a stage to form firm conclusions about such a process" [[1]]. The evolutionary excerpts raise, as a hypothesis, whether increased social contact "emotionally program[med]" rats' expression of an inherent capacity for social tolerance, possibly involving "a relationship-induced expression of previously unexpressed genes" [[12, 14]].

#### 3. Key Dimensions

**Mechanism and content.** Programming results from verbal, nonverbal and behavioral interactions [[1]]; it labels behaviours as good and to be fostered or bad and to be discouraged, leaving the individual with responses on a spectrum from over-compliance to counterpositioning [[15]]. One excerpt describes "environmental family attitudes about right and wrong" and a message that "you are the one who is the cause of the problem" as among the variables that "can go into the programming" [[22]].

**Awareness.** "People are not conscious of most of the emotional programming that occurs, but it affects myriad automatic behaviors that govern much of people's lives, for better and for worse," and automatic responses have interconnected emotional, feeling and subjective cognitive components [[1]].

**Levels of functioning affected.** Programming "appears to affect a child's biochemical, physiological, psychological, and relationship functioning" [[5, 7]]; it involves cortical and subcortical processes [[23]].

**Timing.** The "most intense programming occurs in a person's parental triangle during development" [[1]]; programming occurs through ongoing interactions during developmental years, and "some programming may occur during intrauterine development" [[5, 7]].

**Differential programming of siblings.** Differences in the intensity of family triangles result in siblings being programmed differently; parents typically focus more on one child, and chronic anxiety drives the more intense focus, not the reality needs of the child [[5, 7]].

**Reciprocity.** The child "triggers emotionally programmed reactions in the parent simultaneously with the parent triggering emotional programming in the child" [[8]]; the more parents focus on a child out of their own needs and fears, the more the child focuses automatically on the parents, a reciprocal and ongoing process that may be harmonious or contentious [[6]].

**Social cues.** Four "social cues" — parental attention, approval, expectations and distress — are described as consistent elements of the programming, to which all human beings react [[6]].

#### 4. Relationship to Other Bowen Concepts

- **Differentiation of self / level of differentiation:** emotional programming is proposed as a link between role differentiation and level of differentiation [[3, 11]], and "level of differentiation is created by multigenerational emotional programming" [[21]]. Anxiety-driven parental overinvolvement constrains the child's ability to formulate thoughts, opinions and ideas on their own, producing heightened reactivity to approval combined with unsureness about one's own ideas, or "the reactive opposite, which is arrogance" [[6]].
- **Multigenerational transmission process:** as above [[4, 16, 17]]; the degree of fusion in a parental triangle "cannot be explained adequately by looking at just two generations," and emotional programming "comes down through the generations in myriad forms that with research can be defined objectively" [[8]].
- **Individuality and togetherness:** programming "occurs in the context of the interplay between the counterbalancing life forces of individuality and togetherness," and variations in that balance may explain variation in intensity of programming and in capacity for self-regulation [[8]]; a source adds that programming creating differences between species would have to differ somewhat from programming creating variation within a species [[14]].
- **Chronic anxiety:** chronic anxiety drives the more intense focus on a child [[5, 7]]; anxiety is defined as "the emotional responsiveness of a person to situational stress" [[22]].
- **Triangles and fusion:** see [[5, 7, 8, 29]].
- **Emotional regression and emotional objectivity:** grouped with emotional programming as the "three Es" [[10]].
- **Emotions/feelings (ToCE):** it is argued that Barrett's theory offers ideas about how emotional programming impacts the development of emotions/feelings, and that "how to function" is used as one's goals [[9, 13, 20]]; the same author states ToCE "misses the important idea of how, what, and when does one learn about how they should function in their family" [[18]].

#### 5. Clinical Presentation

Sources give concrete patterns. In one, a mother's "fiercely protective attitude" toward her rebellious adolescent son, explained as a deep yearning to make him happy, manifests in ongoing efforts to solve his life dilemmas; she overfunctions, he reports being hassled and fights her on most issues, sometimes pushing her away physically, and feels she wants to control him and does not trust him [[19]]. "This is the type of interaction that emotionally programs the son, and the mother's actions stem from her own emotional programming. For example, it programs the son to be hypersensitive to criticism and for his first response to be oppositional" [[19]]. Another pattern involves a dysfunctional mother (chronically depressed or abusing substances) whose child is highly tuned into her moods and feels responsible for making her happy — "sort of like a parent-focused child" — with the intensity of the fusion programming "a strong interdependence with the mother," later appearing in romantic relationships as feeling like the giving one who does not get enough in return [[29]]. A third describes a mother investing anxiously in her children so that they focus back reactively, programming "a heightened need for attention," with the child's well-being "excessively regulated by the relationship," a process that transfers into other relationships [[23]].

#### 6. Clinical Implications & Therapeutic Approach

The excerpts contain limited explicit technique. One reports Bowen saying, "That which was created in a relationship [emotional programming in the relationship with his mother] can be fixed in a relationship [in the relationship with his wife]," and that the success of a Bowen-theory-based approach "depends being able to change in relationship to important others in the real world" [[25]]. Another illustrates a shift in the author's own thinking achieved by thinking of his mother "as a child of her mother, not just as my Mother," asking, "What choice did Mother have about the nature of her emotional programming?" — an understanding that helped him be "less judgmental and more tolerant" of her traits [[24]]. A training-context excerpt notes that mental health professionals bring their own emotional programming into training, where signals convey that "pre-programmed, natural behaviors are to be left behind" and a new set learned so the trainee can do it "the right way" [[15]]. The evolutionary excerpts note that, for programming to contribute to speciation, it "has to be transmitted with fidelity to future generations," a hypothesis that "treads the borders of current knowledge in biology" [[26, 27]].

#### 8. Gaps & Limitations

The excerpts provided do not contain a single agreed formal definition: the process is characterised alternatively as transmission by relationships and learning [[1]], as a process between generations [[2, 4]], and as the specific effects of parents' interactions on the child's ability to separate emotionally and develop a self [[6]]; the sources do not resolve which is definitional. The sources do not provide outcome research, assessment instruments, or measurement procedures for emotional programming, beyond the statement that it can "with research … be defined objectively" [[8]]. There is an unresolved tension regarding timing and inheritance: one source states most transmission of programmed levels of emotional attachment in humans "appears to occur after a child's birth" [[26, 27]], while another allows that "some programming may occur during intrauterine development" [[5, 7]]. There is also a stated tension between the claim that programming occurs "not genes" [[1]] and the epigenetic and gene-expression hypotheses raised as open questions [[1, 12, 14, 26, 27]]. The excerpts do not describe specific therapeutic techniques for altering emotional programming beyond the general statement that what was created in a relationship can be fixed in a relationship [[25]]. The provided sources do not address cultural variation, gender beyond the observation that the mother "is usually the primary caretaker" [[23]], or the relation of emotional programming to sibling position beyond the differential-focus statements [[5, 7]]. The provided sources contain no information on empirical validation of the concept as a whole.
