## 1. Executive Summary

Emotional programming is defined in the sources as a process of transmission across generations that operates "through the transmission of information across generations in a family by relationships and learning, not genes" [[4]]. A closely related formulation distinguishes it from behavioral transmission: "behavioral transmission and emotional programming both involve learning and relationships, but behavioral transmission usually refers to the content of the information transmitted across generations (such as a specific skill or belief) and emotional programming refers to a process between the generations" [[1]]. Programming is described as "the basis for a behavioral inheritance system that seems as precise and predictable in the transmission of information across generations as the genetic inheritance system" [[1]].

A second, more clinically specific definition states that "emotional programming refers to the specific effects that the nature of the parents' interactions with a child during development have on the child's ability to separate emotionally and develop a self" [[7]]. The sources describe the programming as resulting "from the verbal, nonverbal, and behavioral interactions that occur within and between the generations," with the most intense programming occurring "in a person's parental triangle during development," while siblings and other family members also exert important influence [[4]]. People are "not conscious of most of the emotional programming that occurs, but it affects myriad automatic behaviors that govern much of people's lives, for better and for worse," and it "can intensify or deintensify fear reactions to social stimuli" [[4]].

The sources place emotional programming among what one author calls "the three Es of Bowen theory: emotional regression, emotional objectivity, and emotional programming," stating that "each concept is unique to Bowen theory" and that placing "the term emotional in front of each term anchors them to the bridge—the emotional system—that Bowen theory constructs to link human behavior to evolution and the behavior of other species" [[19]]. Emotional programming is identified as "the first process" discussed among three phenomena or emotional processes described by Bowen theory, and is described as "an important process to consider for understanding the transmission of information across generations" that "may also provide a link between role differentiation and level of differentiation" [[2]].

The sources link emotional programming to the development of basic level of differentiation: "The development of a child's basic level is promoted or constrained by the degree of emotional fusion that exists between parents and child and the associated emotional programming that occurs within the fusion" [[27]]. The child most targeted by anxious parental interaction "inherits the parents' immaturities through what Bowen theory terms emotional programming," and "depending on the intensity of the interactions, one or more children may develop less maturity than the parents through this relationship transmission" [[15]].

Two limits on the concept are stated explicitly in the sources: "The exact ways in which this 'programming' occurs are not well defined" [[5]], and research on the interaction between emotional programming and gene expression "is at too early a stage to form firm conclusions about such a process" [[4]].

## 2. Full Report

### 2.1 Introduction & Definition

The sources present emotional programming primarily as a multigenerational transmission process. One definition states that the phenomenon "occurs through the transmission of information across generations in a family by relationships and learning, not genes," and that "the programming results from the verbal, nonverbal, and behavioral interactions that occur within and between the generations" [[4]]. Emotional programming is also distinguished from behavioral transmission on the basis that the latter "usually refers to the content of the information transmitted across generations (such as a specific skill or belief)" whereas emotional programming "refers to a process between the generations" [[1]].

A second definition locates the concept in the parents' interaction with a developing child: "Emotional programming refers to the specific effects that the nature of the parents' interactions with a child during development have on the child's ability to separate emotionally and develop a self" [[7]]. The same source describes the mechanism as reciprocal: "The more the parents focus on a child out of their own needs and fears, the more the child focuses automatically on the parents. This reciprocal and ongoing process, which can be harmonious or contentious, heightens the child's reactivity to parental attention, approval, expectations, and distress" [[7]].

Emotional programming is enumerated as one of the three phenomena or emotional processes described by Bowen theory under discussion, alongside emotional regression and individuality and togetherness [[2]]. It is also grouped with emotional regression and emotional objectivity as "the three Es of Bowen theory," each described as "unique to Bowen theory," with the qualifier that "the terms regression, objectivity, and programming are used in other bodies of knowledge, but not in the same way Bowen theory uses them" [[19]].

### 2.2 Theoretical Foundations

The sources describe emotional programming as operating at the level of a behavioral inheritance system: "programming is the basis for a behavioral inheritance system that seems as precise and predictable in the transmission of information across generations as the genetic inheritance system" [[1]]. In a discussion of symptom development, the concept is invoked as one of the variables determining symptom evolution in the nuclear family: symptoms have to do with "the kind of programming that went into these two people" and "the programming from the past" [[3]].

The sources place the concept in relation to evolution. Emotional programming is described as a term Bowen theory uses for changes deriving from relationship interaction: in the fox domestication research, "the domestication of the foxes involved both changes in gene functioning and in learning, both of which derived from their relationship interaction with human beings—or what Bowen theory refers to as emotional programming" [[18]]. The "emotional" prefix is described as anchoring the three Es "to the bridge—the emotional system—that Bowen theory constructs to link human behavior to evolution and the behavior of other species" [[19]].

The sources also note a connection to epigenetics while explicitly limiting claims about it: "the exploding field of epigenetics, which examines the interplay between the environment and the switching off and on of genes, opens the door to studying the interaction between emotional programming and gene expression. The research in this field is at too early a stage to form firm conclusions about such a process" [[4]].

A related but non-identical usage appears in the sources, in which "programming" refers to conventional theory being "programmed into a head," so that "this head is not able to see anything except the programming" [[10]]. This is a distinct usage from emotional programming as a family process, and the sources do not equate the two.

### 2.3 Key Dimensions

**(a) Verbal, nonverbal, and behavioral channels.** The programming results from "the verbal, nonverbal, and behavioral interactions that occur within and between the generations" [[4]]. Another source describes programming occurring "through multiple verbal and non-verbal message[s]" that "labels certain behaviors as good and to be fostered and others as bad and to be discouraged" [[13]].

**(b) Location in the parental triangle.** "The most intense programming occurs in a person's parental triangle during development, but siblings and other family members have an important influence on it as well" [[4]].

**(c) Non-conscious, automatic operation.** People "are not conscious of most of the emotional programming that occurs, but it affects myriad automatic behaviors that govern much of people's lives, for better and for worse," and it "can intensify or deintensify fear reactions to social stimuli"; moreover, "automatic responses have interconnected emotional, feeling, and subjective cognitive components" [[4]].

**(d) The four social cues.** "Myriad variations of content exist in this child focus, but these four 'social cues' are consistent elements of the programming that occurs": parental attention, approval, expectations, and distress [[7]]. All human beings react to these cues, and "ideally they facilitate smooth social interactions," but "anxiety-driven parental over involvement with a child not only heightens his or her reactivity to the cues, but it also constrains the child's ability to formulate thoughts, opinions, and ideas on their own" [[7]]. The sources add that "heightened reactivity, for example, to approval coupled with unsureness about one's own ideas (or the reactive opposite, which is arrogance)" is a further consequence [[7]].

**(e) Cortical and subcortical processes.** What is described as happening between parents and children as emotional programming "involves cortical and subcortical processes" [[21]].

**(f) Physiological/biological programming of anxiety level.** People "can be 'programmed' on biological and psychological levels to have a certain level of anxiety," though "the exact ways in which this 'programming' occurs are not well defined"; "based on the physiological relationship between mother and fetus, some 'programming' may occur in the womb. Most of it occurs while children are growing up" [[5]]. Similarly, an individual growing up in a nuclear family is "'imprinted' with a level of chronic anxiety characteristic of this branch," and this absorption "seems to be based on the physiological programming or conditioning of one person by another that occurs through prolonged association, and on the imparting and incorporation of attitudes and beliefs that foster anxiety in oneself or others" [[12]].

**(g) Content dimension — how to function.** Emotional programming is described as the medium through which "the individual learns how to function in the family via emotional processes and programming," with "how to function" serving as the individual's goals, and "the concepts come[ing] from how the family talks (or doesn't) about themselves and how family members should behave" [[6]].

### 2.4 Relationship to Other Bowen Concepts

**Differentiation of self / basic level.** Emotional programming is directly tied to the development of basic level: "The development of a child's basic level is promoted or constrained by the degree of emotional fusion that exists between parents and child and the associated emotional programming that occurs within the fusion" [[27]]. Emotional programming "may also provide a link between role differentiation and level of differentiation" [[2]].

**Multigenerational transmission process.** Emotional programming is described as part of the multigenerational transmission process; the sources state that "the amount of 'self' a child has developed by the time he leaves home is the outcome of a multigenerational transmission process" [[27]], and that emotional programming is "an important process to consider for understanding the transmission of information across generations" [[2]]. A diagram of "the shower of dots" is described as "an attempt to help students of Bowen theory think beyond genetic transmission," symbolizing "an emotional process or emotional field that flows down the generations" [[8]]. Elsewhere the sources refer to "the subtle and obvious programming occurring through the generations and determining so much of human behavior" [[28]].

**Nuclear family emotional process / family projection.** The child most targeted by parents' anxious interactions "inherits the parents' immaturities through what Bowen theory terms emotional programming," and "depending on the intensity of the interactions, one or more children may develop less maturity than the parents through this relationship transmission" [[15]]. Individuals bring "emotional programming or automatic ways of perceiving, feeling, and behaving from his or her experience in the multigenerational family into his or her nuclear family, such as projecting versus absorbing tension in the triangle" [[17]].

**Emotional fusion.** Programming is described as occurring "within the fusion" between parents and child [[27]]; a mother-child example describes parent and child becoming "entangled in each other's emotions" [[21]].

**Triangles.** The most intense programming occurs "in a person's parental triangle during development" [[4]], and emotional programming includes ways of behaving such as "projecting versus absorbing tension in the triangle" [[17]].

**The three Es.** Emotional programming is grouped with emotional regression and emotional objectivity as concepts "unique to Bowen theory" [[19]].

**Symptoms.** Symptom evolution in the nuclear family is described as having "to do with the kind of programming that went into these two people" and "with the programming from the past" [[3]].

### 2.5 Clinical Presentation

The sources describe emotional programming as appearing in patterned family interactions. In one example, a mother's "legacy" from "this emotionally intense interactive process was emotional programming of a heightened sensitivity to the feelings of others and feelings of guilt if she could not find ways to relieve the distress of others," which "had a strong impact on all her children, but especially the one with whom she was most fused"; this "programmed into my brother a very strong dependency on our mother to help him to manage his feeling states, which is part of what erodes people's 'self'" [[8]].

In another example, a mother's "emotional programming in her functional oldest position contributed to an attitude that, when she had her own family, she would do absolutely everything she could to shape a family life that would make things turn out right for her children, one far removed from what she experienced growing up" [[14]].

The sources describe the outcome in the child when a mother "invests a lot in her kids in an anxious way": "the kids focus back on her in a reactive way. This programs, for example, a heightened need for attention in the child. Parent and child become entangled in each other's emotions. The process may be subtle or obvious. The child may feel and act as if he or she is not getting adequate attention, but the child becomes addicted to attention and thus can be quick to feel inadequately attended to. A child's sense of emotional well-being becomes more dependent than it would otherwise have been on what the mother does and says. The child's well-being becomes excessively regulated by the relationship, and that process transfers into other relationships" [[21]].

Programming is also described as producing a range of behavioral postures: "The individual, depending on his reactivity in the system, is left with multiple responses on the spectrum from over-compliance to counter-positioning. These responses may vary throughout the life cycle of the individual and his family" [[13]]. Patterns such as domination/adaptation are described as "a product of biology and programming obtained in one's family," with the observation that "much of what the children see becomes duplicated or mirrored in subsequent relationships" and that "the mechanisms for the transmission of this duplication may operate not only in the lessons taught but by the lessons caught in the emotionality of the situation" [[22]].

### 2.6 Clinical Implications & Therapeutic Approach

The sources state that "it is this emotional programming that those of us who choose the mental health profession bring into a training context," where "one quickly picks up multiple signals, both verbal and non-verbal, presenting the message that all those pre-programmed, natural behaviors are to be left behind and a new set of behaviors learned," and "this new programming then will allow the trainee to do it the 'right way'" [[13]].

The sources report Murray Bowen's statement: "That which was created in a relationship [emotional programming in the relationship with his mother] can be fixed in a relationship [in the relationship with his wife]" (personal communication on many occasions) [[16]]. The same passage states that "the success of a Bowen-theory-based approach depends being able to change in relationship to important others in the real world," and that had the man's mother still been alive, "he could have made changes with her as well" [[16]].

The sources also state that "of the various sources of anxiety, however, self-imposed psychological pressure and pressure from the relationship system are particularly important to recognize because people's anxious responses to these stresses can be modified," and that "some of the physiological components of a person's anxious response to stress can be modified by psychological changes in that person"—for example, "if a person becomes more sure of 'who he is' and 'what he stands for,' his physiology will be less reactive to emotional pressure from others" [[5]].

Regarding responsibility, one source states that "unless one assumes responsibility for one's own life course, progress in psychotherapy is impossible," with the corollary that "the better differentiated a person is the more responsibility he or she can carry without becoming dysfunctional" [[28]].

### 2.8 Gaps & Limitations

- **Mechanism:** The sources state that "the exact ways in which this 'programming' occurs are not well defined" [[5]].
- **Epigenetic interaction:** "The research in this field is at too early a stage to form firm conclusions about such a process" [[4]].
- **Transmission of chronic anxiety and patterns of emotional functioning:** The sources state that "the theory is less specific about whether chronic anxiety and patterns of emotional functioning are transmitted across generations," that "many Bowen theorists believe they are," and that demonstration of a heightened stress response in an adult mouse "is not equivalent to the phenomenon of chronic anxiety as described by Bowen theory" [[27]].
- **Measurement or assessment of emotional programming:** The provided sources do not address this point.
- **Specific clinical techniques directed at reversing emotional programming:** Beyond the general statements quoted in section 2.6 [[16, 5, 28]], the provided sources do not address this point.
- **Timing/developmental stages beyond early childhood:** The sources state only that "most of it occurs while children are growing up" and that some "may occur in the womb" [[5]]; the provided sources do not otherwise address this point.
- **Related terms not equated in the sources:** [[30]] describes "automatic emotional reactivity" as "an important component of the 'emotional guidance system' of an individual" without using the term emotional programming; the provided sources do not explicitly equate the two. Likewise, [[20]] discusses "epigenetic programming of maternal behaviors" without using the term emotional programming.
