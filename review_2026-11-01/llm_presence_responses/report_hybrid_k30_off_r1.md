## 2. Full Report

### 2.1 Introduction & Definition

The sources describe emotional programming as transmission across generations occurring "by relationships and learning, not genes" [[1]]. It results from "the verbal, nonverbal, and behavioral interactions that occur within and between the generations" [[1]].

Kerr's definitional contrast is explicit: "Behavioral transmission and emotional programming both involve learning and relationships, but behavioral transmission usually refers to the content of the information transmitted across generations (such as a specific skill or belief) and emotional programming refers to a process between the generations" [[2, 3]].

A second, more clinically focused definition appears in a different source: "Emotional programming refers to the specific effects that the nature of the parents' interactions with a child during development have on the child's ability to separate emotionally and develop a self" [[7]]. This source also states that "the more the parents focus on a child out of their own needs and fears, the more the child focuses automatically on the parents," a "reciprocal and ongoing process, which can be harmonious or contentious" [[7]].

Guerin's text describes programming as occurring "through multiple verbal and non-verbal messages" that label "certain behaviors as good and to be fostered and others as bad and to be discouraged" [[16]].

### 2.2 Theoretical Foundations

The sources place emotional programming inside the multigenerational transmission process. Bowen theory's concept of that process "is broader than what biologists generally mean by behavioral transmission," and "includes the emotional programming of one generation by the generations preceding it" [[3, 10]]. The programming is described as "the basis for a behavioral inheritance system that seems as precise and predictable in the transmission of information across generations as the genetic inheritance system" [[2, 3]].

The concept is presented as one of three processes discussed in relation to evolutionary theory: emotional programming, emotional regression, and individuality and togetherness [[5, 10]]. It is described as "an important process to consider for understanding the transmission of information across generations" and as one that "may also provide a link between role differentiation and level of differentiation" [[5, 10]].

One textbook author groups emotional programming with emotional regression and emotional objectivity as "the three Es of Bowen theory," stating that "each concept is unique to Bowen theory" and that "no other theory in psychology or biology has anything comparable," while noting that the terms "are used in other bodies of knowledge, but not in the same way Bowen theory uses them"; placing "emotional" in front of each term "anchors them to the bridge—the emotional system" [[11]].

On Bowen's own usage, Galloway states that "Bowen only used 'emotional(ly) program(med)' twice in his collected papers" and that he wrote the multigenerational transmission process was the "least detailed" and "needs the most attention" [[15, 17]].

The sources also locate emotional programming relative to epigenetics: "the exploding field of epigenetics, which examines the interplay between the environment and the switching off and on of genes, opens the door to studying the interaction between emotional programming and gene expression," though "the research in this field is at too early a stage to form firm conclusions about such a process" [[1]].

### 2.3 Key Dimensions

**(a) Site and timing.** Programming occurs in "the ongoing interactions parents and other family members have with a child during his developmental years," and "some programming may occur during intrauterine development" [[4, 6]]. "The most intense programming occurs in a person's parental triangle during development, but siblings and other family members have an important influence on it as well" [[1]].

**(b) Domains affected.** Emotional programming "appears to affect a child's biochemical, physiological, psychological, and relationship functioning" [[4, 6]]. Automatic responses are said to have "interconnected emotional, feeling, and subjective cognitive components" [[1]]. The process "is anchored in the emotional system but includes complex feeling and psychological components" [[4, 6]].

**(c) Differential programming of siblings.** "Differences in the intensity of family triangles result in siblings being programmed differently," since "parents typically focus more on one child than on the other(s)," and "chronic anxiety drives the more intense focus, not the reality needs of the child" [[4, 6]].

**(d) Consciousness.** "People are not conscious of most of the emotional programming that occurs, but it affects myriad automatic behaviors that govern much of people's lives, for better and for worse," and "can intensify or deintensify fear reactions to social stimuli" [[1]].

**(e) The four social cues.** The child's reactivity is heightened to "parental attention, approval, expectations, and distress," described as "four 'social cues'" that "are consistent elements of the programming that occurs"; all human beings react to these cues, and "ideally they facilitate smooth social interactions," but "anxiety-driven parental over involvement with a child not only heightens his or her reactivity to the cues, but it also constrains the child's ability to formulate thoughts, opinions, and ideas on their own" [[7]].

**(f) Balance of life forces.** "Emotional programming occurs in the context of the interplay between the counterbalancing life forces of individuality and togetherness in parent-offspring relationships," and "variations in the particular balance of these forces can explain variation in the intensity of the emotional programming that occurs, as well as variation in the capacity for self-regulation" [[9]].

### 2.4 Relationship to Other Bowen Concepts

- **Multigenerational transmission process:** the process "includes the emotional programming of one generation by the generations preceding it" [[3]]. Multigenerational emotional process "is anchored in the emotional system and includes emotions, feelings, and subjectively determined attitudes, values, and beliefs that are transmitted from one generation to the next," and "this transmission is assumed to occur primarily through relationships" [[15, 17]]. The concept of the multigenerational transmission process also "describes how different children in the family are more or less emotionally involved in the family emotional processes, resulting in the children having different levels of differentiation of self" [[13, 17]].
- **Differentiation of self:** one hypothesis states that "level of differentiation is created by multigenerational emotional programming" [[22]]. Differentiation and anxiety are also named among the variables controlling emotional dysfunction, with anxiety triggering symptoms [[21]].
- **Triangles:** intensity of family triangles determines differential programming of siblings [[4, 6]]; the degree of fusion in a parental triangle "cannot be explained adequately by looking at just two generations" [[9]].
- **Individuality and togetherness:** programming occurs in the interplay of these counterbalancing forces [[9]].
- **Emotional system:** the process is "anchored in the emotional system" [[4, 6]]; the "emotional" prefix anchors the three Es to "the bridge—the emotional system" [[11]].

### 2.5 Clinical Presentation

The sources give several illustrations:

- **Overfunctioning with a rebellious son:** a mother explains "her fiercely protective attitude toward him as resulting from a deep yearning to be able to make him happy"; "she overfunctions with the son," who "reports feeling his mother hassles him," fights her "on most every issue," and sometimes pushes her away physically. "This is the type of interaction that emotionally programs the son, and the mother's actions stem from her own emotional programming. For example, it programs the son to be hypersensitive to criticism and for his first response to be oppositional" [[20]].
- **Attention addiction:** when "a mother invests a lot in her kids in an anxious way, the kids focus back on her in a reactive way," programming "a heightened need for attention in the child"; the child "becomes addicted to attention and thus can be quick to feel inadequately attended to," and "the child's sense of emotional well-being becomes more dependent than it would otherwise have been on what the mother does and says," so that "the child's well-being becomes excessively regulated by the relationship, and that process transfers into other relationships" [[23]].
- **Parent-focused child:** where a mother is dysfunctional (e.g., "chronically depressed or abusing substances"), fusion may take the form of a child "being highly tuned into the mother's moods and feeling a lot of responsibility for making her happy"; "the intensity of that fusion programs the child into a strong interdependence with the mother, showing itself in strong need for her attention and later in romantic relationships," and such a person "often feels like the giving one in the relationship, not getting enough in return" [[26]].
- **Attitudes about right and wrong:** among "the various variables that have to do with the programming of people" is "environmental family attitudes about right and wrong and a notion that sort of conveys to this one, you are the one who is the cause of the problem" [[21]].
- **Behavioral labelling:** programming via verbal and non-verbal messages leaves the individual, "depending on his reactivity in the system," with responses "on the spectrum from over-compliance to counter-positioning," which "may vary throughout the life cycle" [[16]].
- **Multigenerational reach:** one author reflects that a grandmother's decline "may have been related to feeling overwhelmed by the birth of a fourth child," and that the mother's "sensitivity to the unhappiness and distress of others might have been intensified by responding to her mother's distress," asking, "What choice did Mother have about the nature of her emotional programming?" [[24]].

### 2.6 Clinical Implications & Therapeutic Approach

The sources offer limited explicit guidance, but several statements bear on clinical work:

- The programming process "comes down through the generations in myriad forms that with research can be defined objectively," and "seeing the multigenerational process provides a much-needed broad perspective for what is unfolding in the here and now" [[9]].
- Bowen is quoted: "That which was created in a relationship [emotional programming in the relationship with his mother] can be fixed in a relationship [in the relationship with his wife]"; "the success of a Bowen-theory-based approach depends being able to change in relationship to important others in the real world" [[25]].
- Understanding programming can reduce judgment: one author reports that understanding his mother's programming "helped me be less judgmental and more tolerant of certain traits in Mother that also affected my development," a shift aided by "being able to think about Mother as a child of her mother, not just as my Mother" [[24]].
- Guerin extends programming into professional training: "It is this emotional programming that those of us who choose the mental health profession bring into a training context," in which trainees pick up signals that "pre-programmed, natural behaviors are to be left behind and a new set of behaviors learned," so "this new programming then will allow the trainee to do it the 'right way'" [[16]].
- A separate source, speaking of family *emotional process* rather than emotional programming specifically, states that "within training programs a modified version of family emotional process is transferred into the classroom," and that "the lower the level of functioning of the participant the greater the likelihood this transference will become a subtle determinant of whether individuals complete a program or not" [[28]].
- Galloway argues that Barrett's theory "provides some insight into how 'emotional programming' can impact the development of emotions/feelings," proposing that "the sensing of how one should function becomes one's goals," and that "most of this learning is nonconscious" [[13, 19]]. The same author states that this theory "misses the important idea of how, what, and when does one learn about how they should function in their family" [[18]].

**On emotional programming and gene expression (an unresolved area in the sources):** [[1]] states programming occurs "not genes" while noting epigenetics "opens the door" to studying interaction between programming and gene expression but is "at too early a stage to form firm conclusions" [[1]]. Kerr raises the possibility of "a relationship-induced expression of previously unexpressed genes" in Calhoun's rat experiment, while noting that such programming "would be occurring within an existing generation of rats rather than representing multigenerational emotional programming" [[12, 14]]. He further states that for emotional programming "of one generation to contribute directly to evolution and speciation, the programming has to be transmitted with fidelity to future generations, a hypothesis that treads the borders of current knowledge in biology" [[27, 29]]. These passages are not reconciled in the sources and are reported here as stated.

### 2.8 Gaps & Limitations

- The sources do not provide a single, formal, verbatim Bowen-theory definition of "emotional programming" beyond the varying formulations quoted above (a "process between the generations" [[2, 3]]; "the specific effects that the nature of the parents' interactions with a child during development have on the child's ability to separate emotionally and develop a self" [[7]]). The variation across sources is noted rather than resolved here.
- No source describes a specific clinical protocol, technique, or manualized procedure for altering emotional programming. The sources speak generally of change occurring "in relationship to important others in the real world" [[25]].
- How, when, and by what mechanism learning of "how one should function in the family" occurs is described as a gap in another theory by Galloway [[18]]; the sources do not supply a detailed account of the mechanism themselves.
- The relationship between emotional programming and gene expression is explicitly described as unsettled and "at too early a stage to form firm conclusions" [[1]], and as "treading the borders of current knowledge in biology" [[27, 29]].
- The sources do not address measurement instruments, prevalence, cultural variation (beyond a brief example that the concept "be good" "would have been defined very differently across a group of families of different backgrounds" [[19]]), or outcome research on modifying emotional programming.
- The provided sources do not address this point: whether emotional programming is reversible, and under what conditions.
