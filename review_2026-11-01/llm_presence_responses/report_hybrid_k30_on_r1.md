# 1. Executive Summary

The sources define emotional programming most explicitly by contrast with behavioral transmission. Both "involve learning and relationships," but "behavioral transmission usually refers to the content of the information transmitted across generations (such as a specific skill or belief) and emotional programming refers to a process between the generations" [[27, 28]]. Emotional programming is located within the multigenerational transmission process: "The multigenerational transmission process includes the emotional programming of one generation by the generations preceding it," and "The programming is the basis for a behavioral inheritance system that seems as precise and predictable in the transmission of information across generations as the genetic inheritance system" [[28]].

The sources describe emotional programming as operating on both biological and psychological levels: people "can be 'programmed' on biological and psychological levels to have a certain level of anxiety," and while "some 'programming' may occur in the womb," "most of it occurs while children are growing up" [[2]]. The exact mechanisms are explicitly left undefined: "The exact ways in which this 'programming' occurs are not well defined" [[2]].

In nuclear-family terms, a person growing up is "imprinted" with a level of chronic anxiety characteristic of that branch, an absorption "based on the physiological programming or conditioning of one person by another that occurs through prolonged association, and on the imparting and incorporation of attitudes and beliefs that foster anxiety in oneself or others" [[9]]. The programming is described as influencing which *category* of dysfunction develops — "learning based on childhood experience appears to be the most important influence on the category of clinical dysfunction (physical, emotional, social)" — while "genetic predisposition to a disease, however, can be strong enough to override relationship programming" [[11]].

Content of programming named in the sources includes "environmental family attitudes about right and wrong" and a conveyed notion that "you are the one who is the cause of the problem" [[1]]; "early life indoctrination or programming" concerned with "attention to body functioning" in people who develop physical illness [[17]]; parental "emotional responsiveness or turned onness to girls and not to boys or the opposite," which "can go much faster" the process [[14]]; and the transmission of values "across the generations" [[24]]. "Relationship patterns are determined by the programming from the past" [[19]]. The sources also use "programming" for mental/cognitive set: "the way the human head is programmed," seen as "a product of the learning and also the emotional system" [[15]], and conventional theory "programmed into a head" so that "this head is not able to see anything except the programming" [[22]]. At the species level, the sources raise but do not resolve whether "emotional programming" from increased social contact occurred within one generation of rats, "rather than representing multigenerational emotional programming" [[30]].

# 2. Full Report

## 2.1 Introduction & Definition

The clearest definitional statement is relational rather than substantive: emotional programming is distinguished from behavioral transmission by what is transmitted versus what kind of process is involved. "Behavioral transmission and emotional programming both involve learning and relationships, but behavioral transmission usually refers to the content of the information transmitted across generations (such as a specific skill or belief) and emotional programming refers to a process between the generations" [[27, 28]].

Its placement is explicit: "The multigenerational transmission process includes the emotional programming of one generation by the generations preceding it" [[28]]. The product of this programming is described as "a behavioral inheritance system that seems as precise and predictable in the transmission of information across generations as the genetic inheritance system" [[27, 28]].

The sources do not provide a formal, standalone definition of "emotional programming" beyond these statements; the mechanisms are expressly stated to be undefined [[2]].

## 2.2 Theoretical Foundations

The concept is described as belonging to the emotional system, not the intellectual system. The sources state that "the programming . . . of one's head, I would see as a product of the learning and also the emotional system" [[15]]. Elsewhere, "programming" appears alongside biological/anxiety processes: people "can be 'programmed' on biological and psychological levels to have a certain level of anxiety" [[2]].

The sources also describe programming as a multigenerational phenomenon transmitted "not in a consistent pattern, not in a genetic pattern, but in transmission, some kind of transmission" [[24]], and as one of the variables determining how symptoms evolve: "It has to do with the kind of programming that went into these two people" and "the programming from the past" [[5]].

The sources use emotion in a broad, evolutionary sense — emotion "can refer to all processes that guide the individual automatically within an environment," including "genetic factors, experientially acquired mechanisms" [[10]] — and Bowen is described as having extended Darwin's idea of emotionality until "emotion synonymous with life force" [[8]]. These passages define *emotion*, not programming; the sources do not explicitly connect that definition to "emotional programming."

## 2.3 Key Dimensions

**Biological/physiological versus psychological.** Programming occurs on both levels [[2]]; it is described physiologically as "the physiological programming or conditioning of one person by another that occurs through prolonged association" [[9]].

**Timing.** "Based on the physiological relationship between mother and fetus, some 'programming' may occur in the womb. Most of it occurs while children are growing up" [[2]].

**Mechanism stated as undefined.** "The exact ways in which this 'programming' occurs are not well defined" [[2]].

**Content: attitudes and beliefs.** Absorption of anxiety "seems to be based on . . . the imparting and incorporation of attitudes and beliefs that foster anxiety in oneself or others. Incorporating an attitude such as 'I am inferior' can create anxiety for oneself; incorporating an attitude such as 'I am the greatest' can create problems for others" [[9]]. Named family attitudes include "attitudes about right and wrong" and a conveyed notion that "you are the one who is the cause of the problem" [[1]], and "attention to body functioning" in families of physically ill members [[17]].

**Category versus specific symptom.** Learning from childhood experience is described as "the most important influence on the category of clinical dysfunction (physical, emotional, social) that develops," while genes "seem to have less influence on the category of dysfunction" but influence the specific symptom: "if a person with a genetic predisposition to cancer (symptom) is 'programmed' during childhood to act out his anxiety (social dysfunction) rather than to internalize it, he may never develop cancer" [[11]].

**Parental selectivity.** A described accelerator: "the programming of parents, mostly the female, is that she has some kind of a emotional responsiveness or turned onness to girls and not to boys or the opposite, and when this occurs, then the process can go much faster" [[14]].

**Relationship patterns.** "Relationship patterns are determined by the programming from the past" [[19]].

**Cognitive/head programming.** Programming is also used for the conceptual set with which one perceives: conventional theory "is programmed into a head and then this head is not able to see anything except the programming" [[22]]; "the way the human head is programmed" [[15]].

**Within-generation versus multigenerational.** The sources distinguish the two: increased social contact in rats raised the question "Did the increased social contact emotionally program the expression in the rats of an inherent, previously unexpressed capacity for social tolerance?"; such "emotional programming from increased social contact would be occurring within an existing generation of rats rather than representing multigenerational emotional programming" [[30]]. The sources add that "the emotional programming creating this variation would have to differ somewhat from the programming that creates variation within a species" [[30]].

## 2.4 Relationship to Other Bowen Concepts

**Multigenerational transmission process.** Emotional programming is described as contained within it [[28]], and the transmission process is listed as describing "the patterns of emotional process through multiple generations" [[26]].

**Chronic anxiety.** Programming is described as setting a level of anxiety [[2]], and as the means by which a person is "imprinted" with a characteristic level of chronic anxiety [[9]]. Chronic anxiety and automatic reactivity are described as interconnected: "The more 'inherent' anxiety, the more individuals and relationships tend to be governed by automatic emotional reactivity" [[21]].

**Differentiation of self.** The sources describe symptom intensity as controlled by "the degree of differentiation of self, the degree of anxiety" [[1]], and state that "the intensity of the process is governed by the degree of undifferentiation, by the degree of emotional cutoff with families of origin, and the degree of stress in the system" [[3]].

**Symptom development.** The choice among marital conflict, spouse dysfunction, or projection to children "depends on the way these people were programmed" [[24]]; symptom evolution "has to do with the kind of programming that went into these two people" [[5]].

**Emotional system.** Programming of the head is described as a product of learning and the emotional system [[15]]; the emotional system is described as governing "the 'dance of life' in all living things" and emotional illness as "a dysfunction of the emotional system" [[4]].

## 2.5 Clinical Presentation

The sources describe programming as appearing as (a) a characteristic level of chronic anxiety [[9]]; (b) a category of dysfunction — physical, emotional, or social [[11]]; (c) in physically ill members, an early focus on body functioning, with "reasonable flexibility" allowing minor symptoms that shift "toward symptom development in the dysfunctional spouse" when anxiety increases [[17]]; (d) in socially ill members, "one whose thinking being focuses more in the relationship system than in self" [[24]]; and (e) in patterns that are "as fixed in families as any characteristics in families . . . almost as defined as a fingerprint," determined by "the programming from the past" [[19]].

## 2.6 Clinical Implications & Therapeutic Approach

The sources state that systems thinking "puts in a number of variables for understanding it, and most important of all, it puts in the same number of variables for dealing with it in therapy" [[5]]. They caution that measures which lower anxiety intensity — individual therapy, group therapy, religious or charismatic movements — "does not increase the level of differentiation nor slow down the intensity the evolution of this across multiple generations" [[5]].

On modifiability, the sources state: "Some of the physiological components of a person's anxious response to stress can be modified by psychological changes in that person. For example, if a person becomes more sure of 'who he is' and 'what he stands for,' his physiology will be less reactive to emotional pressure from others" [[2]]. They also describe anxiety reduction "based on learning rather than on emotional or physical distance," achieved by an intellectual decision to engage avoided situations and "to tolerate the anxiety associated with not doing things one normally does to reduce anxiety in oneself," which "can, if done repeatedly over a long period of time, lead to a reduction in one's level of chronic anxiety" [[29]].

**The sources do not explicitly state whether emotional programming itself can be undone or reversed**, nor do they describe a therapeutic procedure directed at emotional programming as such.

The equation of the two is an inference, not a stated equivalence.
- **No defined clinical protocol** for emotional programming appears in the excerpts; the therapeutic statements address anxiety reduction and differentiation rather than programming directly [[2, 5, 29]].
- **Reversibility not addressed.** Whether emotional programming can be modified, and to what degree, is not stated in the provided sources.
- **Measurement not addressed.** The provided sources do not address how emotional programming is assessed or measured.
- **Open empirical question.** The rat research is presented as raising a question rather than answering it [[30]], and the passage on species-level variation is truncated mid-sentence ("does re") [[30]].
- **No explicit treatment** in these excerpts of emotional programming in the extended family beyond the stated intention to "leave the notion that it comes out of the multi-generational past" [[24]].
